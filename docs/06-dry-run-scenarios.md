# Dry-Run Scenarios

**Education only — this guide is never medical advice.** This chapter teaches how to draft, verify, and run *practice scenarios* — fictional, clearly-labeled rehearsal exercises for orientee learning — including AI-assisted drafting. Scenarios here teach thinking and communication on a pretend floor; they are not simulations of care, they contain no clinical instructions you could act on, and nothing here replaces your facility's simulation process, policies, educator, or clinical team.

Dead time is real: pre-rounding lulls, a quiet hour on night shift, the last hour of Friday. A verified scenario in your pocket turns dead time into the best teaching slot of the week — with zero risk to anyone, because nothing in it is in care.

## What a dry-run scenario is

Not a simulation. Simulation belongs to your facility's own process and is someone else's job. A dry-run scenario is 10–20 minutes of structured pretend, run by you, about *deciding and communicating*:

- A fictional patient is presented in one sentence of category, never detail: *"an adult recovering from a routine procedure, day two, steady yesterday, something changed since lunch."* No values, no names, nothing a real chart would say.
- The orientee decides and speaks: prioritizes, calls, handoff, chart note — the choices that are always about *order, words, and escalation*, never about facts.
- The scenario ends in a debrief, and the debrief is half the value.

The safety rule with teeth: **a scenario you cannot strip of all clinical specifics is a scenario you either re-draft or skip.** Teach ordering, wording, escalation, and confidence; leave the medicine out entirely.

## The build-and-verify loop

This is [chapter 5](05-ai-for-lesson-plans.html)'s workflow, tightened for scenarios.

**1. Name the target.** "Orientee practices: opening a call in one clean sentence + one clear ask." One skill per scenario; two makes both shallow.

**2. Draft with a structure prompt.** Template to adapt (paste nothing real):

```
Education only. Draft a 15-minute PRECEPTOR PRACTICE SCENARIO for a
[unit] orientee. Fictional and generic — every detail must be a
category, never a value: no lab numbers, no medications, no orders,
no patient identifiers, nothing clinical to act on.

Skill to practice: [e.g., "requests help early and gives a clear
one-hundred-word synopsis"].

Frame: A fictional patient described only in categories. The scenario
presented, what the orientee must say or decide, a scripted fork
point (you play role X), 3 debrief questions, and a one-line "what
good sounded like."

Constraint: any line that looks like clinical guidance must be
replaced by "[PRECEPTOR FILLS]" — I own every fact.
```

The `[PRECEPTOR FILLS]` convention keeps the scenario's honesty visible: any fact the orientee would need to *know*, you supply from facility-approved sources — or the scenario doesn't use facts at all.

**3. Verify your `[PRECEPTOR FILLS]` and de-fictionalize.** Every blank: fill only from policy, educator materials, or things already true about your unit's *processes* (who to call, how the phone works, what the form is named) — all of which you already know cold. Then delete any fiction that could be mistaken for real care situation detail.

**4. Rehearse once, alone, out loud.** Two minutes: do the fork work? Does the debrief piece flow? Scenarios that rehearse fine tend to run fine; scenarios that seem weak out loud are weak, and we are honest people.

**5. Run it on real dead time, off-unit-protectively.** Launch as a named game: *"Five-minute practice, pretend, decision-only — nothing here touches the desk or the record."* Play your role; let them struggle a bit in the deliverable; their struggle is allowed and expected.

**6. Debrief, four questions, their words first.** What was hard? What did your fork choice produce? What would you do differently — concretely? What should we *practice next* — name the next scenario together, theirs.

**7. Log and re-stack.** One row in the [weekly teaching plan](../worksheets/weekly-teaching-plan.html): scenario, target skill, went/how. Good ones go to your personal pocket — the half-dozen you can reuse next cohort, always remembering every good one stays fictional and hers-or-his to maintain.

## The scenario library (six shapes to cycle)

| Shape | You play | Orientee practices |
|---|---|---|
| The vague caller | A distracted someone on the phone who is vague about urgency | Getting the concrete ask, drawing out the concern, making the summary crisp |
| The two-good-options | A colleague offering two defensible next moves | Picking one out loud, saying the why in a sentence, committing |
| The awkward handoff | The receiving nurse who keeps interrupting | Holding their structure and finishing their report anyway |
| The reluctant ask | A gruff charge nurse ("I'm sure it's fine…") | Asking anyway — politely, once, clearly |
| The teach-back | A brand-new orientee of *their* | Teaching yesterday's lesson, one skill, with the why |
| The fifty-minute Monday | Nobody — it is a solo drill | Sequencing a standard start-of-shift run out loud in two minutes, then defending the order |

Nothing in that table contains facts — every scenario tests *sentence-making and spine*, which is exactly the layer new grads are actually weak at, which is exactly the layer your judgment can check without a policy look-up.

## If the orientee pushes back

Some orientees find role-play cringy even after a fair try — that is a preference, not a character flaw, and the fix is the same as any preference: make the trade explicit.

- *The ask:* "These run ten minutes and they're imaginary. Fair trade for us both — I can't fabricate a crisp call pressure on a real morning, and you can't be first-day good at asking. Four of my last six orientees swore by them by week three."
- *The concrete time:* offer once per week, not every shift, at a fixed moment.
- *The opt-out:* they can trade it for a real-time observed version ("we'll do the real thing together, and you'll hear my debrief"). Choice replaces the power struggle, and no struggle occurs.

## The one-line summary

A dry-run scenario is a four-step loop — target, verified draft, rehearsal, debrief — that converts dead time into the highest-safety teaching you have, as long as you keep every fact out, every frame fictional, and every debrief theirs.

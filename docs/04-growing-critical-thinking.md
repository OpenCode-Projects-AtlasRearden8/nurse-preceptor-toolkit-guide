# Growing Critical Thinking

**Education only — this guide is never medical advice.** This chapter teaches questioning and role-play habits that help a new grad think out loud and reason safely. It teaches no clinical content and makes no claims about what any value, sound, or situation means. Nothing here replaces your facility's protocols, your state practice act, or your clinical team.

The endgame of precepting is not an orientee who knows the answers. It is an orientee whose thinking you can see, check, and trust — and eventually an orientee who does that checking on themselves while you watch from across the room. The tool for that is not more lectures. It is questions, asked in a predictable shape.

## The question ladder

Borrow the shape of flat-to-deep questions and climb gently. Every rung makes thinking visible; each rung up demands a bit more of the orientee's working memory, so time them with the week number, not with your impatience.

| Rung | Shape | Example | Best weeks |
|---|---|---|---|
| Recall | "What would you look for…?" | "What's in your handoff for this room?" | 1–2 |
| Process | "Walk me through how you decide…" | "How do you decide what to check first when you walk in?" | 2–4 |
| Compare | "What are your two choices for X, and what tips you?" | "Two ways to sequence this room's tasks — which and why?" | 3–5 |
| Hypothetical | "What would change your plan if…?" | "What would make you reorder this run if the door phone rang?" | 4–6 |
| Defend/revise | "Play devil's advocate on your own call." | "Argue the other side of that decision for me." | 5+ |
| Mentor-flip | They generate the question for *you* | "Ask me the hardest question about last shift." | 6+ |

The climb rule: one rung up from wherever they answered last time, never two. A question that lands just above their reach stretches; one that lands way above it teaches only that questions are ambushes.

## The three anchor questions

When you have no time and no plan, these three work on nearly any shift and take seconds:

1. **"What would you do if I weren't here?"** — the standing flip for any question they ask you. Their answer comes first, always, then you respond to the answer rather than the fear.
2. **"What are you watching for next, and what would change your mind?"** — keeps a picture of *trajectory* on both screens: what they expect and what would falsify it.
3. **"What's the part you're least sure about?"** — names the fuzzy edge out loud. Half-formed worry is welcome report (day-one script from [chapter 1](01-setting-a-new-grad-at-ease.html)); this question makes it culturally safe every day thereafter.

## Role-play as the zero-risk practice floor

Role-play questions are rehearsal with no patient in the room — the whole reason they work. The smallest useful form is one question, one answer, in the ten-minute debrief. The bigger forms, below, are worth scheduling once weeks three-ish and six-ish from your [weekly teaching plan](../worksheets/weekly-teaching-plan.html):

- **The phone-call role-play.** You play the caller; the orientee has to open the call, give their read, and make one clear ask. Run it twice: once smooth, once deliberately awkward caller.
- **The two-doors drill.** You describe a fork ("two things this run could do next"), they pick a door and defend it, then you take the other door and they argue *against* their own answer. Defending the wrong side is where the reasoning gets tough — and where it's fun.
- **The teach-back flip.** They teach one skill to you as if you were the brand-new hire, including the why. Teaching exposes reasoning gaps that answering does not.
- **The hand-back question.** At the end of any hard role-play: "What made that hard, and what would make it less hard next time?" — they design their own next drill.

Role-play works only when it stays clearly pretend: name the game before you start ("this is practice — nothing here touches the desk, the record, or anyone real"), and debrief warmly. The launch rule from [chapter 6](06-dry-run-scenarios.html) applies to any scenario you draft.

## If they freeze

Freezing in a drill or a real-time question is a normal stress response, not a thinking failure — treat it as information and lower the temperature instantly:

1. Shrink the question by half ("just the first step — what happens first?").
2. Offer two concrete options ("more like A or more like B?").
3. Step one rung down the ladder and practice there until fluency returns, then re-approach tomorrow.

If freezes repeat across several days, that is a [difficult week](08-surviving-the-difficult-week.html) signal to work with deliberately, not a verdict on them or your teaching.

## The question bank, thirteen good starters

Copy these into the [role-play question bank](../worksheets/role-play-question-bank.html) worksheet and add your unit's own over time:

1. "Walk me through your first five minutes of this shift."
2. "What annotation are you watching, and what would change your mind?" — phrase with whatever your unit actually calls it.
3. "If I called in sick right now, what's the first thing you'd do differently?"
4. "Teach this task back to me as if I'm new."
5. "What two things could go wrong with this run, and what would they look like from you?"
6. "Play devil's advocate on your own last handoff."
7. "What's the question you'd be embarrassed to ask me — ask it anyway."
8. "Which task today took longer than it should have, and what was the bottleneck?"
9. "What would the *most experienced* nurse you know do differently just now?"
10. "Where did you double-check yourself, and what tipped you?"
11. "That call you needed to make: how would you open it in one sentence?"
12. "What's one thing you'd want me to watch you do tomorrow on purpose?"
13. "What did you notice that I didn't comment on? (Tell me even if it's small.)"

Two or three per shift is plenty — questions are expensive for both brains. The [question bank](../worksheets/role-play-question-bank.html) worksheet gives a running log so none of them cannibalize each other week over week.

## The one-line summary

Critical thinking grows where thinking is visible, safe, and slightly above comfortable during every exercise — questions, rung by rung, with role-play as the risk-free rehearsal room in between real shifts.

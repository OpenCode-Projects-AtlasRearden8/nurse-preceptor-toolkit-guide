# Setting a New Grad at Ease

**Education only — this guide is never medical advice.** This chapter covers the human-factors side of day one: scripts, structure, and expectations that lower a new grad's panic level so learning can happen. It does not cover clinical care or any facility competency process, and never overrides your policies or your clinical team.

A terrified brain does not learn. It defends, it hides, and it nods. Most of day one is not teaching content — it is making the classroom survivable so teaching can start.

## The fear math, plainly

Your orientee walked in carrying every rumor they have ever heard: that preceptors wash people out, that asking one dumb question marks you, that the first med error is career-ending. They have not yet learned that week one is supposed to feel humbling for everyone. You cannot delete the fear. You can make it smaller and give it somewhere to go:

- Name it first, out loud, before they have to perform bravery.
- Give every fear a matching plan (a script, a form, a time of day it gets discussed).
- Remember yourself in week one. Being the person you needed is the entire job description in one sentence.

## Day one, in four easy sentences

These four go early, roughly in this order, before any assignment is discussed:

1. **"I'm not here to wash you out. My job is to make this job yours in [unit's orientation length] weeks, and to be glad while it happens. Nobody fails a first shift for being a first shift."**
2. **"I precepted before and I remember what it felt like. My goal by the last week is that you're telling me what to do, not the reverse."** — names the endgame on day one; every later act of autonomy reads as progress, not burden.
3. **"There are no questions I'll judge. Fastest way to make me look twice: not asking again about something you were unsure of once."**
4. **"If something feels wrong — a value, a sound, a smell, a vibe, anything you can't quite name — I want the half-formed sentence, not the finished one. Vague worry counts as report from you."**

## The rest of day one

- **Names and tours before tasks.** Bathrooms, supply rooms, break room, the fridge protocol, where the coffee lives. Nobody can concentrate on content while their bladder is a mystery.
- **Their story, not just yours.** One question does it: "What made you pick [specialty]?" You'll use this answer for eight weeks.
- **Set the daily check-in before the first shift ends.** "Last ten minutes of every shift, we debrief: one thing you did well, one thing you'd do differently, one question you're carrying home. I'll bring my list too. Same format every day, so no surprises."
- **Show the map.** Hand over the unit's competency checklist, the *real* expected timeline, and what "ready" looks like on this unit in plain words (write their words down in [preceptor-setup](../worksheets/preceptor-setup.html)).
- **Day one homework: one physical skill they'll teach back tomorrow.** Choose something small and physical. Ending day one feeling competent is the point, not a bonus.

## The first week, shaped

| Day | Theme | What you deliberately demonstrate |
|---|---|---|
| 1 | Names, rooms, scripts above, one teach-back skill | Normalizing questions |
| 2 | Watch-me shift: full assignment, you talk through choices | The thinking between the tasks (see [chapter 2](02-teaching-without-rescuing.html)) |
| 3 | Do-together shift: they prime, you check | Every task has a checking point |
| 4 | Watch-them shift with training wheels on | Checking, not hovering |
| 5 | Their shift, your shadow; full end-of-week conversation | The week-one recap script below |

## Week-one recap script (end of week one)

Have it in [preceptor-setup](../worksheets/preceptor-setup.html) already; say it out loud:

> "Here's what I watched this week: you learn fast and you don't fake it, which is my favorite combination. Here's the bar for week two when it comes to [one specific behavior]. Here's one stretch I want you to attempt on your own: [one specific, small, safe stretch]. Same deal as this week: everything gets said straight, everything gets said kindly, and nothing here is a grade on you as a person. It's just the next step. Questions?"

## Small during-the-shift moves that buy big ease

- **Narrate your own aha moments.** "Huh, that's smaller than I expected" teaches faster than any lecture.
- **Laugh early and often** about your own learning stories (the burned-out-lunch, the wrong-ending phone call). Humor is the fastest normalizer in any room.
- **Never contradict them in front of others; never quiz cold in front of others.** Public surprise feels like a gotcha to a Week-Two brain.
- **Protect one full no-questions break** by at least day three, and make sure they *take* it. Modeling sustainability is teaching too.
- **End every teaching moment with the door open:** "Anything still fuzzy? Even the fuzz counts." The silence afterward is not a good sign; it is a busy one.
- Learn their *coffee order* and their reading pace. Small, specific noticing is what "I see you" looks like in a hospital.

## What to do when ease is not enough

Some orientees start under their fear and can't climb out on scripts alone. The [difficult week](08-surviving-the-difficult-week.html) chapter handles the rough shapes — slow start, tearful afternoon, Friday despair. The short version here: ease is not manufactured from cheerfulness. It is manufactured from humility plus a plan plus a visible rail to hold onto. Be humble, plan visibly, and let the rail be you for exactly as long as the rails are needed.

## The one-line summary

Day one is not about proving anyone ready. It is about proving that learning here is safe, structured, and honest — so the brain turns from defense to absorption and the eight weeks can actually begin.

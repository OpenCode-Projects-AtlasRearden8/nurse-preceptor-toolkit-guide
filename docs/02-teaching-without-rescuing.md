# Teaching Without Rescuing

**Education only — this guide is never medical advice.** This chapter covers how to hand responsibility over in safe increments without either hovering or dropping someone. It does not cover clinical care, and nothing here replaces your facility's competency process, your state practice act, or your clinical team's judgment about what a preceptee may do independently.

The classic preceptor trap is not cruelty, it is gravity: it pulls you toward doing, explaining, and re-doing — because that is faster today. Rescuing keeps your shift tidy and their growth flat. This chapter is the counter-gravity: a plan for giving the job away on schedule.

## The autonomy ladder

Precepting works when you are clear about which rung both of you are standing on *today*, and explicit when the rung changes. The ladder below is a coaching tool, not a clinical rule; your facility's policy decides what an orientee may do and document at each stage.

| Rung | What it looks like | Your sentence announcing it |
|---|---|---|
| 1. I do, you watch | You perform, narrating the thinking out loud | "Today I run the show out loud. Watch for my choice-points, interrupt anytime." |
| 2. We do, side by side | They act, you act alongside; errors surface harmlessly in parallel | "We'll do the same tasks next to each other. Same job, twice done." |
| 3. You do, I check | They do it fully; you verify at each of the same fixed checkpoints | "This one is yours, start to finish. I'll check in at the exact same three points every time." |
| 4. You do, I'm nearby | They carry the task; you embed elsewhere on the unit | "Game on. I'm in [room X] doing [task]; call me, or I'll call you at [time]." |
| 5. You do, I hear about it | They handle it end to end; you learn in the debrief | "Handle it your way. Tell me how it went at lunch check-in." |

Clone this table into your [weekly teaching plan](../worksheets/weekly-teaching-plan.html) and move tasks across it as the weeks pass. A task is not "learned" — it is *at rung 4 as of Tuesday*, which is a much more useful sentence.

## The three handing-over sentences

Whatever the task, the mobility happens in words. These three cover almost every moment:

1. **"Your call first."** — the standing rule. Before you answer any orientee question, flip it one rung: "What would you do if I weren't here?" They answer, then you respond to *their* answer, not to the raw situation. (More question tools in [chapter 4](04-growing-critical-thinking.html).)
2. **"Talk me through it."** — the thinking-made-visible request. It converts silent action into checkable reasoning without stopping the work.
3. **"Go, and I'll come."** — the release. Naming the safety net (physical or by phone) is what makes a stretch safe to try; the net is the freedom.

## When to step in (the only four reasons)

Not rescuing has a hard edge: there are moments you *must* step in, instantly and without apology. Keep the list short so it stays sharp. Intervene immediately when:

1. **Imminent harm** — harm is seconds away and uncorrectable afterward. Move first, explain later, and do explain (a clean "I did that so we'd both see the moment" debrief preserves trust).
2. **The freeze** — they've stopped and can't resume. A frozen orientee needs direction, then a debrief, then a plan for the *next* freeze, not embarrassment now.
3. **The confident wrong turn** — they are moving with purpose in a wrong direction. Fast calm interruption: "Pause one second — walk me through your next two steps." Their spoken plan shows you *and* them where the fork was.
4. **The paper never lies later** — something will be documented, signed, or scheduled that would harm them or someone downstream. Catch it at the desk, not at the audit.

Everything else — slowness, fumbles, ordering problems, awkward calls you already know how they'll end — is deliberate learning surface. It feels worse to watch and it works better.

## The rescue audit (a five-minute Friday habit)

Once a week, replay your shifts honestly and count rescues:

- How many times today did I *start* doing rather than coach, when stepping back was actually safe?
- What did each rescue save me? What did each cost the orientee and next week?
- Which rung-3-or-4 tasks did I quietly pull back to rung 1 this week — and was that a safety call or a speed call?

Log the pattern in the [weekly teaching plan](../worksheets/weekly-teaching-plan.html). Two honest entries will recalibrate you more than any article. Notice especially the rescues that were for *your* comfort, not their safety; those are the growth area for preceptor-you.

## The debrief habit that makes stepping back survivable

Stepping back works only when the retrieval loop works. The daily ten-minute close is the whole trick (set up in [chapter 1](01-setting-a-new-grad-at-ease.html)):

1. **They go first, always:** "How'd it go? What's your read?" — their read before your read.
2. **One true thing said straight:** "The handoff you built at 1500 held up completely."
3. **One next-attempt named:** "Tomorrow: same task, and I'd like your call at the fork without checking in with me first."
4. **Their takeaway recorded in their words.** Motto: their notebook, their handwriting, your presence.

## Holding the difficult middle weeks

Weeks three to five are where gravity wins most battles: assignments thicken, orientees slow down, new tasks stack up. The rescue temptation is at maximum exactly when stepping back matters most. Plan for it:

- Pre-decide the week's ladder in the Sunday [weekly teaching plan](../worksheets/weekly-teaching-plan.html), so mid-shift decisions are recoveries rather than improvisations.
- Trade speed for surface: on a heavy shift, fewer tasks at rung 4 beats many tasks at rung 2.
- Tell them the plan before the shift ("today I'll be a step behind you on purpose"), so distance reads as design, not dislike.

## The one-line summary

Rescuing is invisible generosity that quietly keeps the job yours. The autonomy ladder, three handing-over sentences, and four step-in reasons convert that gravity into a designed transfer — while on purpose, on schedule, and without a single hard stop being necessary.

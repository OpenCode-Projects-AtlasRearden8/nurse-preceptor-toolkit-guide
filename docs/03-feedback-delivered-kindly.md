# Feedback Delivered Kindly

**Education only — this guide is never medical advice.** This chapter teaches conversation habits for feedback — what to say, when, and in what tone — for precepting relationships. It does not cover clinical care, formal evaluations, or any disciplinary process, and never overrides your facility's policies or HR processes.

Feedback is the cargo of precepting. The knowledge exists in you; feedback is how it crosses the gap. Deliver it clumsily and the cargo drops in the water. Deliver it kindly and specifically and the trip takes a week instead of eight.

## The kind-does-not-mean-soft myth, retired first

Kind feedback is not gentle feedback. The kindest thing you will do all year is say a true thing plainly, at the right moment, in a form they can act on. Padding truth ("great job, but...") makes them guess forever. Withholding truth ("it's fine") hand-stamps a bad habit as permanent. Both feel easier today and both betray the person. True-plus-kind beats either alone; it is a skill, and like every skill in this toolkit it has a shape.

## The shape: bar, gap, next attempt

Every piece of corrective feedback worth giving has three parts, in this order:

1. **Name the bar.** State the expected behavior plainly. "In a good report, I'll hear the current problem, the trend, and what you want next."
2. **Name the gap against that bar, behavior-specific.** "That report gave me the trend but not the ask. I had to guess what you wanted me to watch for next."
3. **Name the next attempt.** "Tomorrow, end every handoff with 'what I need from you next' — even if it's just 'keep an eye on this.' I'll have your back on the words."

The rule that keeps it kind: **critique behaviors, spoken in the language of the bar, ending in a plan.** Never a personality, never a comparison to another person, never a verdict on their future.

## The moment rules

| Moment | What helps | Why |
|---|---|---|
| In the middle of a task | Fix, don't lecture: redirect in five words or fewer | Teaching while doing has a working-memory budget of nearly zero |
| Right after a task | Deliberate, curious, two-way: "walk me through what you were thinking between X and Y" | Their model shows you where the fork was; the fix lands twice as deep |
| End of shift debrief | Confirmation of a strength (one true, specific thing), one gap, one next attempt | The daily rhythm makes hard items normal instead of catastrophic |
| Weekly recap | Trends, not incidents; the week in aggregate with the bar and the stretch ahead | Point-in-time stings fade; named arcs build a professional self |
| Same-day, never later than end of shift | Corrective items appear within hours of the behavior | Overnight, a gap stops being correctable and starts being a habit |
| When you're angry or exhausted | Nothing. Debrief briefly "as scheduled" and carry the hard item to a rested you | A reserved preceptor keeps the trust; a tired one can spend it in a sentence |

Also kind: praise in private, correct in private, celebrate in public. The private part is not kindness to them; it is kindness to their standing with a new team — theirs to own, never yours to narrate.

## Copyable openers for the hard ones

- The slipped-same-thing: *"Remember we talked about ending handoffs with an ask? I noticed we slid off that this afternoon. Walk me through what got in the way…"*
- The confident-wrong-call: *"You moved fast and with real conviction on that one. Walk me through the reasoning, because your next step surprised me…"* (This merges [chapter 2](02-teaching-without-rescuing.html)'s step-in rule with feedback: interrupt curious, not hot.)
- The motivation slide: *"The energy I saw in week one isn't what I'm seeing today. I'm asking as your teammate, not your evaluator: what's the honest read?"*
- The preceptor-caused one, said early and cleanly: *"I set that up badly this morning — I was Rescue Monster and I didn't need to be. Reset button, starting now."*
- The Friday row for the [weekly plan](../worksheets/weekly-teaching-plan.html): *"Bar we set Monday, gap I saw, attempt for next week."*

## The receiving half — teach it too

Your orientee copies what they see, not what you say. Narrate your listening skills out loud so they learn the receiving half along with the giving half:

1. **Their default surprise** — say it straight once: "That one surprised me, and I'm going to think on it before I answer you." That is professional, not weak.
2. **Their feedback to you** — ask for it weekly and reward it: "What should I do differently for you? I'm keeping a real list" (keep one; write in it in front of them).
3. **Ask-permission feedback lines:** "Can I give you the rough now and the tidy after lunch?" and "Want the two-minute version or the walk-and-talk version?" Consent-prep is one of the most quietly teachable partnership moves you own.

If they can both give and receive feedback like this by week eight, they will lead some day. Teach backward: this is the finishing move, and it is also the feeder skill for everything else.

## When feedback becomes formal (the boundary)

Plain and early beats elegant and late:

- Your job is *formative*: coaching toward the bar. If you sense you are carrying materials meant for a formal process, say so in daylight terms to your orientee and loop your unit's educator early.
- Never let a surprise develop. Formal-only findings should never land on someone who has not heard them from you in behavior-first shape week after week.
- The paper trail ([hours log](../worksheets/precept-hours-log.html)) is your memory aid; *their* memory of your words matters more. Nothing about the formative channel stops you from being warm; formality only means the stakes arrive named.

## The one-line summary

Kind feedback is true + specific + next-attempt-shaped, in the right moment, mostly in private — and the orientee who learns to receive it well ends up more prepared than the orientee who only ever heard praise.
